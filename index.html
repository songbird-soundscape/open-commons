    A proposed teaching philosophy

    Share what we know. Learn it together. Keep the thinking human.

    A proposed teaching philosophy for a rapidly changing social and technological landscape, centred on the relationships that hold a research community together.

    We now have more power to generate, connect, and share ideas than at any point in history. Education should use that power openly. That means less guarding of "my idea" and more collaboration: among specialists across fields, across institutions, across generations, and with the next generation of students as our research partners from the start.

    Where this comes from

    Back to the Lyceum

    This whole endeavor is inspired by something very old. For the ancient Greeks, there was no wall between geology, biology, astronomy, and ethics. It was all natural philosophy: one long conversation about how the world works and how we should live in it.

    Get back to the basics: seminar, symposium, and mentorship.

    Read more: the Lyceum, then and now

    Aristotle's Lyceum ran on conversation, often while walking its covered walkways, and on close mentorship. Its members gathered observations of animals, plants, and places and pooled what they found. Out of that shared work, Aristotle's student and successor Theophrastus wrote one of the first systematic studies of plants.

    The tools have changed. Open data, AI, and linked hubs can do what the Lyceum's library and collections did, at a scale the Greeks never imagined. What's worth recovering is the spirit: knowledge as a shared conversation across fields, and learning done together, in person, alongside someone who knows more than you and cares that you learn it.

    Open knowledge

    Free articles and courses, cooperation over competition, and hubs that link the fields.

    Open by default

    Knowledge should be free to everyone who wants it

    Knowledge that could improve people's lives or the health of the environment should be shared as soon as possible. Publishing costs money, and public budgets should cover it, so that any interested person can read any article or book they'd like, the same way we fund roads and libraries.

    Open courses, too

    Reading lists and lecture subjects shouldn't be proprietary either. If you have a good idea for a course, you lecture it well, and your students learn from it, share it with everybody. MIT OpenCourseWare and Harvard's free online courses already do this. They put their best teaching right out there, and it only adds to their reputation.

    Sharing the content doesn't weaken rigor. Lectures, readings, and syllabi can be open to all, while the assignments and exams stay fresh every year.

    The spirit of it

    Cooperate and collaborate, not compete and eliminate.

    Why wouldn't that be possible?

    Too much of academic life runs on scarcity: guarding data, racing to publish, competing for the same grants. The tools we have now reward the opposite: ideas multiply when they're shared, and they stall when they're kept to ourselves.

    Unified, not centralized

    Knowledge hubs, linked like a living encyclopedia

    Centralizing knowledge and unifying it are different things. Centralizing puts everything in one place under one owner. Unifying leaves knowledge with the experts who make and understand it, and connects it so it reads as a whole.

    Read more: how hubs work

    Experts keep doing their own work and keep their own data, but they link their databases, visualizations, and explanations into public hubs. Each hub connects to the neighboring fields it touches, to the labs doing the research, and to the educators explaining it.

    Finding out that something exists is half the fun, and also half the battle. It can take years to discover who works on a question you care about. A drawn link between two fields costs almost nothing, and could start a conversation years sooner.

    Centralized

    All knowledge flows into one place. One gatekeeper decides what's kept, who sees it, and what it costs. If the center fails or closes its doors, everything goes with it.

    Unified

    Knowledge stays with the people who make it. Shared links and standards connect it into one map, and anyone can follow the connections. No single owner, and no single point of failure.

    A template, not just a website

    The hub's most transferable value is its pattern. It works as a teaching tool, and it also works as a style template for connecting digitally with people on similar projects. Sociologists Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer called objects like this boundary objects: things that different communities can each use in their own way, while still holding them in common. Their example was a natural history museum where amateurs and professionals built a shared collection.

    Anyone can make a hub. The recipe is simple:

    1. A core question people actually care about.
    2. Layers from the fields that help answer it.
    3. Links outward to bordering fields, labs, educators, and elders.
    4. A review loop where students check and improve the content, with credit.
    5. An open license so others can copy the pattern for their own field.
    Open, with care: how hubs are moderated

    Hubs are public and meant to be safe for all viewers, from schoolchildren to elders. Every contribution is reviewed before it appears. Where a subject is difficult by nature, it is presented with care, context, and respect for the people reading it.

    Learning and research

    Learning that rewards and challenges, students improving AI-drafted knowledge, figures before chapters, the lab as a mentoring network, and elders on the record.

    Zone of proximal development

    Rewarding, challenging, and worth doing

    Students should get right into doing what rewards them and also challenges them. Learning should feel worth doing, not wear students down. Psychologist Lev Vygotsky called the space between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help the zone of proximal development. Work there stretches students without losing them.

    Mentors keep the match right, raising the challenge as skill grows. The chart below is similar to psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's model of challenge and skill: too easy and we get bored, too hard and we get anxious. Try it below.

    Homework that matters

    AI drafts. Students correct. Everyone learns.

    When experts use AI to build hubs, the result is mostly right, but not entirely. Here, AI's failings make for good learning: students take in the content from what's right, and build critical thinking by finding and fixing what isn't.

    The assignment is simple: AI made this, now make it better. Students check each claim against the scholarly literature, correct and cite what they find, and add their own insight. Then they present the changes they've made to classmates and the hub's working group, along with projects they've added to the hub. They can work alone or in groups, in the afternoons and evenings after the day's work.

    Read more: an example from plant phylogenies

    Take a module for exploring plant phylogenies, where patterns in plant evolution become visible that an entering undergraduate would rarely find alone. There are far too many plants for one expert to proofread every species entry, and much of the life-history information comes from Wikipedia. That's excellent work for a young student.

    Figures before chapters

    See the whole, then the detail

    AI makes it easy to turn complex subject matter into figures: maps, trees, timelines, and networks that anyone can explore. People take in and remember a good figure far better than the same idea spread across chapters of text. Psychologists call this the picture superiority effect, and learners do best with words and pictures together.

    The goal is to condense a concept to its most succinct expression, while keeping the granular detail close at hand: a figure you can open layer by layer, down to the original sources.

    The same rule applies as for text: a figure built with AI is a draft, and students check it against the sources. Drifting Through Deep Time ↗ puts 540 million years of continents, plants, and people into one globe and one tree.

    Read more: the research behind it

    Pictures are remembered better than words (Nelson, Reed & Walling 1976). Information held both as words and as images is easier to recall than either alone (Paivio's dual coding theory, 1971). People learn more deeply from words with pictures than from words alone (Mayer, Multimedia Learning, 2009).

    Research comes first, not last

    The lab as a mentoring network

    Research shouldn't wait until a student's final years. It should start early and follow their interests. Students should rotate through several specialists to discover what they love doing, instead of filling their schedule with electives they aren't sure they'll use.

    Think network, not hierarchy. Mentoring runs in many directions, and the roles shade into one another as a gradient rather than stacking as rungs. PhD students mentor master's students and undergraduates alike. Everyone can host visitors, though master's students and advanced undergraduates field most of the visiting public, and undergraduates take the bulk of welcoming prospective students.

    Everyone in the lab is both learning and teaching. And because AI is still wrong some of the time, there is always meaningful work to do: checking, sourcing, testing, and making sense of things.

    Occasional Regular Main role Direction help and knowledge flow

    Select anyone to see what they give and what they get. Help rarely flows equally both ways: a professor gives a PhD student far more than the reverse, but the PhD student still helps.

    Before it's lost

    Get our elders on the record

    Senior scholars and practitioners carry a view of the world that took a lifetime to build. Experts should compile what they know while they still can. We should interview them about what they value, how they see their fields, and the ideas they never got to study. Then we can invite them to review the hubs and modules and tell us whether they look right.

    Their unfinished questions become a template for the next generation's research.

    Pathways

    A foundation year, a shared first semester, and halls full of conversation.

    A new front door

    A foundation year before anyone has to choose a major

    Imagine a publicly subsidized season, or a whole year, of work and service. Young people live together at low cost, grow food, cook, and care for animals. They spend their days alongside students, graduate students, and professors. They learn practical and social skills many people never got growing up, and they see up close what different kinds of work look like.

    Read more: how the year works, and who it fits

    The work offsets the cost. Scheduled hours of weeding, cooking, and butchering, with the meat science program in on it, run a farm-to-cafeteria operation that feeds the campus. In the first two semesters, a student may take one class per term if they want. Graduate students teach those classes, which helps fund their own research. In their off time, students can join study groups and work through the basics with good online modules. Choosing to do that is a good sign of the drive they'll need later.

    The year also fits the person. Someone who grew up knowing plants or birds from their family shouldn't wait in line. Move them quickly into hands-on work, like bird banding, so a strength they arrived with becomes a skill. Everyone has their own strengths, and college should be shaped around them.

    Nobody has to bet four years on a guess. Pick a path below to see how it might play out.

    For every undergraduate

    Semester one: learn to teach, think, and tend

    Every undergraduate takes one combined survey course in ethics and pedagogy, informed by modern thinking in philosophy of mind and phenomenology. Pedagogy is at its heart, because in this system everyone teaches: undergraduates host visitors, mentor newer students, and explain their work in seminars.

    Life skills are taught as real disciplines with real experts. That means culinary arts rather than just cooking, gardening informed by horticulture, livestock farming informed by rangeland management, and carpentry, metalworking, and auto maintenance in a shared makerspace and shop. Wellness, from exercise and yoga to nutrition, is part of every day.

    Tend
    What the buildings are for

    Fill the halls with conversation

    This is the Lyceum part. If explanations can live in interactive maps, videos, and hubs, then a lecture hall doesn't need to hold a hundred half-asleep students hearing what they could have read. Some lecture courses will always make sense. But the rooms could also host symposia, seminars, and talks all day long.

    Open to the public, but at what cost?

    The seminars, symposia, and talks that fill these buildings could be open to anyone, and recorded for the hubs. That raises an open question: should members of the public pay a small fee to attend, and should students? Each option has trade-offs.

    Standards and habits

    Real standards in your own words, and habits for an examined life.

    Rigor

    Real standards, measured against the work

    Openness doesn't mean anything goes. To restore the value of a degree, you should have to really try to get in. Entry is a handwritten short-answer and essay exam, or an oral exam, on 100-level material the student has studied hard. Admission is measured against a standard of work, not against other applicants. Fall short and you can try again, or find a path that suits you better. Once you're in, you get support along the way, so you don't need constant rescuing later.

    We can't keep turning out AI-substituted students. If you can't put an idea in your own words, you haven't learned it yet.

    Students should arrive able to write. AI belongs in the work, for checking, exploring, and visualizing, but it can't replace the person doing the thinking.

    Why wouldn't they cheat?

    Right now the same assignment comes back every year. The answers are already on homework-help sites and flashcard decks, and AI produces the rest in seconds. Teachers end up knowing a student cheated but unable to prove it, sitting through honor-code hearings, and being lied to by students who know they'll get away with it. When a system rewards cheating and rarely catches it, cheating is the rational choice. The answer is better design, not more policing.

    Course content can carry over from year to year. Assignments shouldn't. No assignment should be identical to last year's. Universities can share that load: professors across institutions contributing to a shared, secure bank of fresh questions, and building learning and quiz modules that actually teach, instead of rehashing the basics.

    A philosophy for living and learning

    Freedom takes discipline

    A system with this much freedom only works if people can hold themselves to a standard instead of waiting to be checked. These habits are how. Each one is simple, and each has a reason behind it. Check the ones you already practice to see where to start.

    Daily habits
    Why it helps

    Consequences you can see teach follow-through better than any rule. Seedlings that wilt when you skip a day make the link between effort and outcome concrete, and that habit carries over into study and research.

    Why it helps

    Reasoning is a skill, and like a game it rewards regular play. Platt suggested that one or two gamesworths a day is about as much concentrated thinking as most people can sustain. Many people already give that much effort to puzzles; spent on a question from your hub, the same effort becomes research.

    Why it helps

    Reflection turns experience into learning. Naming what you avoided makes procrastination visible before it hardens into a pattern, and deciding tomorrow's first step makes it easier to start.

    Conscious learning
    Why it helps

    The effort of recalling and reasoning is what builds memory and understanding. Help that arrives too early hands you the answer and takes away the learning. Comparing your attempt with the help shows exactly where your gaps are.

    Why it helps

    Recognizing an idea when you see it feels like knowing it, but it isn't. Pulling it from memory strengthens it, a finding called retrieval practice, and it exposes what's missing while there's still time to fix it.

    Why it helps

    Independence depends on judging your own work accurately. Each comparison with your mentor's judgment calibrates yours, until you can trust it.

    Accountability
    Why it helps

    Explaining forces you to organize what you know and reveals gaps instantly. It also strengthens the community: the person you teach will one day teach someone else.

    Why it helps

    Peers catch your mistakes and you catch theirs, and showing up for others keeps you steady when motivation dips. Choosing to join is itself a sign of drive.

    Why it helps

    Work others can see is work people do carefully. Credit also builds a record of what you've contributed.

    Why it helps

    Honesty lets mentors see how you actually learn, so their feedback fits. It only holds up when disclosure isn't punished, so the system has to make honesty safe.

    Proving it
    Why it helps

    Choosing when you're ready builds self-knowledge, and a fixed standard keeps the result meaningful. Retakes turn failure into information instead of a verdict.

    Why it helps

    Explaining and answering live questions shows understanding no document can fake. It's practice for every seminar, interview, and hard conversation to come.

    Already happening

    None of this has to be invented from scratch

    Pieces of this vision already exist, scattered across field stations, databases, colleges, and courses. The work ahead is to connect them and make them the norm rather than the exception.

        Ethical statement

        The costs of the tools behind this work, and why I use them anyway.

        The costs

        What these tools cost

        This statement represents my personal perspective, which I do not expect or require anyone to adopt for themselves.

        The tools I use to build this work have a destructive side:

        Water

        Data centers use large amounts of water for cooling, and in dry regions they can draw down the local groundwater supply.

        Mining

        Rare earth elements, cobalt, lithium, copper, and tantalum are dug from landscapes at real cost to ecosystems, and too often to the people who mine them, sometimes including children.

        Energy

        Training and running AI, and powering every device, still depends heavily on electricity from fossil fuels.

        Manufacturing

        Making chips consumes huge amounts of energy and ultrapure water, and releases fluorinated gases such as nitrogen trifluoride and sulfur hexafluoride, which trap thousands of times more heat than carbon dioxide and can persist for centuries to millennia.

        Electronic waste

        Devices become obsolete and are quickly replaced. Much of the waste from this turnover is shipped abroad and taken apart by hand, exposing workers to toxic metals.

        Hidden labor

        AI systems rely on low-paid workers who label data and filter out disturbing content, often in the Global South.

        Other people's work

        These models were trained on writing, art, and research made by people who were rarely asked and seldom paid.

        Noise

        Data centers run cooling fans and backup generators around the clock. People living nearby describe a constant hum that disrupts sleep and daily life.

        Local communities

        New data centers claim land and strain power grids, which can raise electricity costs for the people living nearby.

        Most of these costs fall on people and places far from me, who did not choose them. No personal choice settles that. The real answers are collective: fair pay and safe conditions for the people who mine, build and label; consent and payment for the people whose work trains these models; and limits on the water and energy that data centers draw.

        It would be hypocritical to ask others to give these tools up while I continue to use them. Whether or not these means are justified rests on the ends that I hope they facilitate:

        1. the collective ownership of freely shared information, and
        2. the collaborative creation of high-quality educational resources that help people understand ecosystem function, organismal biology, human ecology, and climate processes.

        Part of my reason for creating this work and sharing it openly is to avoid doubling efforts that make use of these tools. When knowledge is shared, the cost of building it is paid once instead of over and over.

        When I looked, there did not appear to be a non-commercialized searchable network of people, organizations, teaching tools, and philosophical dispositions so as to facilitate connection based on curiosity, purpose, and study subject.

        If you know of work like this, please connect. I would much rather join forces than duplicate.

        Get together

        An open invitation to join a network of scholars and knowledge hubs.

        Everyone is welcome

        Join the network

        Scholars, students, educators, elders, and anyone who's curious: bring what you know. Make a hub, join a hub, or link your lab.

        This network is being built carefully, so that it doesn't turn into social media as we've come to know it. It's built for exploring a network of knowledge, not scrolling a feed.

        What you'll find
        • Network exploration: follow the links between hubs, fields, and the people doing the work
        • Open information, free for anyone to read
        • Open-source code that anyone can inspect, copy, and improve
        • Hubs linked across people, fields of study, and institutions
        • Contributions reviewed before they appear, so the network is safe for everyone
        • News and updates posted on the main forum
        What you won't find
        • Passwords or log-ins
        • Ads
        • System emails
        • Status updates
        • Likes
        • Follower counts
        • Addictive scrolling environments
